# What Air Traffic Controllers Actually Do: The Job, the Problems It Solves, and a Day on Position (2026)

An air traffic controller's job is to keep aircraft from hitting each other, or the ground, or vehicles on the runway. They do this by giving every aircraft in their airspace a guaranteed minimum buffer of distance or altitude, while also moving as much traffic as the runways and sky can safely hold. In practice they are the human control plane of a real-time distributed system that, according to the FAA, serves more than 45,000 flights and 2.9 million airline passengers a day. The US version of that system is now short of certified operators, running on hardware dating back to the 1980s, and in the middle of a multi-year rebuild.

## TL;DR

- **What they do:** Controllers keep aircraft separated (typically 3 nautical miles or 1,000 feet in terminal airspace, 5 miles en route). They pass each flight from facility to facility, from clearance delivery and ground control through the tower, approach control (TRACON), en route centers and oceanic control. The FAA Command Center throttles national demand with ground stops and ground delay programs.
- **Day to day:** Shifts rotate around the clock, classically on the "2-2-1 rattler" ending in an overnight "mid". The union contract says controllers should not work more than two consecutive hours on position without a break. Every handoff of a position follows a formal relief-briefing checklist, and workload swings from dead quiet to saturated in minutes during arrival and departure "pushes."
- **Career reality in 2026:** Entry is competitive. You must be hired before age 31, pass the FAA Academy in Oklahoma City (the failure rate was over 30% in FY2024), and retire by 56. The 2025 median pay was $148,080. The FAA had about 11,000 certified controllers in April 2026 against a new target of 12,563, so six-day weeks and mandatory overtime are still common. The DCA midair collision (2025), the Newark radar blackouts (2025) and the LaGuardia runway collision (2026) put workload, staffing and aging equipment at the center of the safety debate.

## Key Findings

- **The core product is separation, not "directing planes."** Everything else (sequencing, rerouting around storms, emergency handling) is built on top of the guarantee that two aircraft never get closer than the applicable minimum. The FAA's rulebook for this is Order JO 7110.65.
- **ATC is a pipeline of specialized facilities.** A single flight from Boston to Los Angeles typically talks to clearance delivery, ground, local (tower), departure (TRACON), several en route center sectors, approach (TRACON), tower, and ground again. Every boundary is a handoff that has to be coordinated.
- **Flow management is capacity management.** When demand exceeds what a runway can accept (because of weather, wind, or staffing), the Air Traffic Control System Command Center holds aircraft on the ground at their origin rather than in the air. To an engineer this will look like backpressure and admission control.
- **Staffing is the binding constraint.** The FAA hired a record 2,235 trainees in FY2026, but training takes years and failure rates are high. GAO (report GAO-26-107320) found the FAA employed 13,164 controllers at the end of FY2025, down from 14,007 at the end of FY2015, while flights grew about 10% to 30.8 million.
- **Recent accidents point at systems, not just individuals.** The NTSB blamed the 2025 DCA collision on airspace design and systemic FAA safety-management failures, with controller workload a contributing factor. At LaGuardia in 2026, one controller was working both local and ground control on a two-person overnight shift.

## Details

### 1. What controllers do and where they sit

**The mission.** BLS summarizes the job as coordinating "the movement of aircraft to maintain safe distances between them." Its list of duties covers directing aircraft on the ground and in the air, controlling all runway and taxiway traffic, issuing takeoff and landing instructions, transferring control of flights between facilities, briefing pilots on weather and runway closures, and alerting airport emergency crews. BLS adds that safety is the primary concern, "but they also must direct aircraft efficiently to minimize delays." That pairing (safe first, then orderly and expeditious) is the classic ICAO and FAA framing.

**Separation standards, the core invariant.** Under FAA Order JO 7110.65, terminal radar controllers using a single airport surveillance radar normally apply 3 nautical miles of lateral separation when targets are less than 40 miles from the antenna, and 5 miles beyond that. En route centers generally use 5 miles. Vertical separation is 1,000 feet, and above FL410 it is normally 2,000 feet even in airspace with Reduced Vertical Separation Minimum (RVSM) rules. Larger wake-turbulence buffers apply behind heavy jets: for example, 6 miles behind a Heavy in some en route cases. On parallel approaches the rules get more intricate. For dependent approaches to runways 3,600–8,300 feet apart, aircraft on adjacent finals need only 1.5 nm diagonal separation. Think of these as hard SLOs that may never be violated. A loss of separation is logged and investigated as an "operational error" even if nothing bad happens.

**The facility types (US / FAA):**

| Facility | Positions | What it handles | Analogy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Airport Traffic Control Tower (ATCT)** | Clearance Delivery / Flight Data, Ground Control, Local Control ("Tower") | Clearance delivery issues the route clearance; ground runs taxiways and ramps; local owns the runways and the few miles around the airport | Edge / ingress controller |
| **TRACON** (Terminal Radar Approach Control) | Departure and approach sectors | Roughly the busy airspace 30–50 miles around major airports: climbing departures, sequencing arrivals onto final | Load balancer in front of the runway |
| **ARTCC** (en route "Center"), 20 in the continental US | High- and low-altitude sectors | Cruise traffic between terminal areas; runs on the ERAM automation platform | Backbone routing |
| **Oceanic** (New York, Oakland, Anchorage centers) | Procedural/oceanic sectors | About 23 million square miles over the Atlantic, Pacific and Arctic, using the ATOP system | High-latitency WAN links |
| **ATCSCC** (Command Center, Warrenton, VA) | Traffic management specialists | National flow management: ground stops, ground delay programs, reroutes | Global rate limiter / orchestration layer |

A real example of the tower–TRACON split: at Newark, BLS-style tower controllers handle the runways, then "controllers at regional air traffic facility known as a Terminal Radar Approach Control (TRACON), take over and help them navigate until they reach cruising altitude." Since July 2024 that TRACON function for Newark has been performed from Philadelphia ("Area C"), not Long Island.

**Oceanic control is a different world.** Over the open ocean there is no radar beyond roughly 250 miles from shore. FAA describes its ATOP system at the three oceanic centers as "a procedural, control-by-exception system… Controller action is required only when aircraft deviate from their flight profiles." Pilots communicate by CPDLC datalink (text messaging between controller and cockpit), with HF radio as the fallback, and ATOP runs a conflict probe with a two-hour predictive horizon. On the North Atlantic, the busiest oceanic airspace in the world, NATS (from Prestwick, Scotland, with Ireland's air navigation service, hence "Shanwick") and NAV CANADA (Gander) share control. NATS says "between two and three thousand aircraft fly across the North Atlantic" every day. Shanwick hit a record 1,805 movements in 24 hours on 9 July 2023, and Gander handled 500,000 flights in calendar 2024. The biggest recent change is space-based ADS-B (Aireon satellites receiving aircraft position broadcasts). It went live on 27 March 2019. NAV CANADA says it cut longitudinal separation "from 5 minutes (approximately 40 nautical miles) to 14-17 nautical miles" and lateral separation "from 23 nautical miles to 15-19 nautical miles for equipped aircraft," while position updates went from every 14 minutes to every 7–8 seconds. This is a good example of better telemetry directly unlocking capacity.

**International equivalents.** Most countries have a single air navigation service provider (ANSP), such as NATS in the UK, NAV CANADA, or DFS in Germany, running the same tower, approach and area-control structure. Europe adds a supranational layer. EUROCONTROL serves as the EU-appointed **Network Manager** (reappointed until 2029). Its Network Manager Operations Centre balances capacity and demand across its 41 member states and allocates departure "slots" much like the FAA Command Center. Each national ANSP staffs a Flow Management Position (FMP) that acts as the interface. NATS runs its FMP at Swanwick and Prestwick, in constant contact with airlines, airports, the military and on-site Met Office forecasters.

### 2. The problems controllers solve

**a) Collision avoidance and separation.** The baseline problem is keeping conflicting aircraft apart. Controllers do it with altitude assignments, vectors (headings), and speed control, plus "visual separation," where a pilot reports traffic in sight and takes responsibility for avoiding it. The DCA accident showed the limits of that last tool. The NTSB found that "the longstanding practice of relying on pilot-applied visual separation (see-and-avoid) as the principal means of separating helicopter and fixed-wing traffic" near DCA "led to a drift in operating practices… that increased the likelihood of a midair collision."

**b) Runway incursions.** These are aircraft or vehicles where they shouldn't be on a runway, and they are the tower's nightmare scenario. On March 22, 2026 at LaGuardia, the tower cleared Air Canada Express Flight 8646 (a CRJ-900) to land on Runway 4. Moments later, with the jet about 100 feet above the ground, the same controller cleared airport fire truck "Truck 1" across the runway; the truck was responding to another aircraft reporting an odor on board. Nine seconds before impact the controller transmitted "Stop, stop, stop, stop. Truck 1." The firefighter heard the warning but didn't know at first that it was meant for them. Both pilots were killed. The NTSB noted the truck had no transponder, which limited the tower's ability to track it on surface surveillance. Only two controllers were in the tower that night, and the local controller was also working ground. The case shows the classic failure mode of combined positions on a quiet overnight shift: one human holding two mental models (airborne and surface) at once.

**c) Weather deviations and rerouting.** Thunderstorms close airspace in real time. En route controllers approve pilot deviations around cells, then have to re-separate everyone who has diverted into neighboring traffic and coordinate with adjacent sectors whose airspace those aircraft now enter. The Command Center may push large-scale reroutes. At the airport level, a Philadelphia tower/TRACON manager described using a "short-term ground stop, which is about 30 minutes-to-an hour" when a thunderstorm is rolling through, to "stop the traffic and give a little break."

**d) Congestion and flow management.** This is where ATC looks most like systems engineering:
- **Ground Delay Program (GDP):** When projected demand exceeds an airport's arrival rate for a long period, the Command Center assigns each inbound flight an Expected Departure Clearance Time (EDCT) at its origin. In one FAA example for LaGuardia (high winds reducing the arrival rate), the maximum delay issued was 74 minutes and the average was 36.
- **Ground Stop (GS):** This is the blunt instrument: flights bound for the affected airport don't depart at all. Local facilities can impose stops of up to 15 minutes without notifying the Command Center, and longer ones go through the ATCSCC.
- **Metering, sequencing and Miles-in-Trail:** Controllers and automation space arrivals so they reach the runway threshold in an efficient, evenly spaced stream. In engineering terms this is token-bucket shaping upstream so the runway never sees a burst.

**e) Emergencies.** Medical, mechanical, fuel, pressurization or security emergencies jump the queue. The controller clears airspace and runway, coordinates with adjacent facilities, and alerts airport rescue and firefighting crews (BLS lists "alert airport response staff in the event of an aircraft emergency" as a core duty). Equipment failures are emergencies for the controllers too. On April 28, 2025, Philadelphia TRACON Area C controllers working Newark traffic lost radar and radio for about 90 seconds because of a burnt copper wire; NATCA said they were "unable to see, hear, or talk to" their aircraft. A second blackout on May 9 was caused by the failure of both primary and redundant communication lines. Several controllers took trauma leave afterward.

**f) Coordination between sectors and facilities.** Every aircraft crossing a boundary is handed off: the receiving controller accepts the radar track, the pilot is told to switch frequency, and responsibility transfers. Letters of agreement between facilities predefine altitudes and routes at the boundaries, much like interface contracts between services. Point-outs, approval requests and inter-facility coordination fill much of a controller's non-radio time.

**g) Communication problems.** Radio is a half-duplex shared channel. "Blocked" or "stepped-on" transmissions (two parties keying at once) and readback errors are a perennial hazard. At DCA, the NTSB found the word "circling" in a traffic call to the Black Hawk was likely inaudible, and the Army crew never heard the instruction to "pass behind the CRJ" because the transmission was stepped on. Controllers counter this with strict phraseology, mandatory readbacks, and, increasingly, datalink.

**h) VFR traffic.** Pilots flying under visual flight rules may not be talking to ATC at all, or only asking for "flight following." Controllers provide traffic advisories workload permitting, and in Class B and C airspace they must actively manage these aircraft alongside airliners. When staffing collapsed during the fall 2025 government shutdown, the FAA prohibited some VFR approaches, parachute operations and photo missions near short-staffed facilities to shed load. That is a clear case of graceful degradation by dropping lower-priority traffic.

**i) Airspace restrictions and military coordination.** Temporary Flight Restrictions (for VIP movements, wildfires, stadiums), special-use military airspace that goes "hot" and "cold", and military helicopters operating in civilian terminal areas all have to be deconflicted. The DCA collision is the starkest recent example. The NTSB found that Helicopter Route 4 was placed too close to the Runway 33 approach path "without adequate safeguards" and that the FAA "failed to act on data and repeated warnings."

### 3. Day-to-day work

**Shift structure.** Facilities run 24/7 or close to it, so most controllers rotate through day, evening ("swing") and overnight ("mid") shifts. The classic pattern is the **2-2-1**, nicknamed "the rattler" because "it can come back and bite the controller": two swing shifts, two day shifts, then a midnight shift that historically began as little as eight hours after the last day shift ended. Controllers like it because it ends in a long weekend (about 80 hours off). An FAA Civil Aerospace Medical Institute laboratory study found participants' sleep fell to about 3 hours just before the midnight shift. The NTSB called for abolishing 2-2-1 back in 2007. Since 2024, an FAA–NATCA agreement requires **10 hours off between shifts and 12 hours off before and after a midnight shift**. That defangs the worst form of the rattler, though rotating schedules remain.

**Time on position and breaks.** The FAA–NATCA collective bargaining agreement (Article 33) says employees "should not be required to spend more than two (2) consecutive hours performing operational duties without a break away from operational areas." Supervisors are responsible for administering breaks. The "should" matters: at short-staffed facilities, controllers report routinely exceeding two hours. The FAA's 2026–2028 workforce plan, as quoted by Fox News, says that improving average controller time on position "from about four hours to more than five hours per eight-hour shift" could increase effective workforce availability enough to meet current staffing targets. That is a productivity lever, but it cuts against fatigue concerns.

**Position relief briefings.** Taking over a position works like a formal on-call handoff with a runbook. FAA procedure has the relieving controller first *preview* the position (review the checklist and status information areas, observe the equipment and traffic). Next comes a *verbal briefing* from the outgoing controller covering traffic, pending actions and equipment status, and then an explicit statement that responsibility has been assumed. Some facilities' local orders require the relieved controller to stay plugged in for a couple of minutes afterward. This matters because FAA research shows a higher percentage of operational errors happens early in a controller's time on position, and the checklist alone is "insufficient for assessing the various states of mind a controller is operating under immediately following a position transfer."

**Workload variability.** Traffic arrives in banks: hub airlines schedule waves of arrivals and departures, and controllers call these "pushes." A sector can go from two aircraft to saturation in ten minutes. Supervisors and controllers-in-charge combine positions when it's slow and split ("de-combine") them when it's busy. The DCA case shows what happens when that doesn't happen fast enough. Investigators said the local controller felt "a little overwhelmed" at 10 aircraft, and about 90 seconds before the collision the controller was working 12 aircraft (seven airplanes and five helicopters) with helicopter and local control combined.

**The toolset:**
- **Radar scopes and automation:** **ERAM** (En Route Automation Modernization), fully deployed across all 20 en route centers by 2015, replaced a 40-year-old host computer. **STARS** is the terminal automation in TRACONs and towers. Both fuse radar and ADS-B surveillance and run conflict alerts.
- **ADS-B:** Aircraft broadcast GPS-derived position. It is now a key surveillance source alongside radar, and space-based ADS-B extends it over oceans.
- **Flight progress strips:** Paper strips in plastic holders are still the norm at many towers. They are being replaced by electronic strips under **TFDM** (Terminal Flight Data Manager). DCA's tower switched in February 2026, and Austin became the 18th TFDM airport in July 2026, with the program targeting nearly 90 airports by 2028.
- **Data Comm / CPDLC:** Text clearances to the cockpit. The tower service sends departure clearances at 65 airports, and as of 2025 the en route service runs across all 20 centers. It isn't used for instructions that require prompt execution, so voice remains primary in tactical situations.
- **Radios, voice switches and phraseology:** Standardized wording ("cleared to land," "line up and wait," "hold short") is the protocol spec. The FAA's rebuild plans 27,625 new radios and 462 digital voice switches.

**Coordination with supervisors and traffic management units.** Front-line managers and controllers-in-charge set the staffing and position configuration and decide when to request flow restrictions. Traffic Management Coordinators at each facility talk to the Command Center, roughly the way an SRE team escalates to a central incident commander.

**Ongoing training.** Certification is facility- and position-specific. A fully certified controller who transfers has to recertify at the new facility; about 1,000 of the FAA's trainees in April 2026 were previously certified controllers in exactly that situation. On top of that come recurrent proficiency training, skill checks and refresher training on new procedures and equipment.

### 4. Career realities

**How to get in (US).** Applicants must be US citizens and under 31 when hired. They must pass a medical exam, a security investigation and the **AT-SA** (Air Traffic Skills Assessment) aptitude test, and they need a year of progressively responsible work experience, a bachelor's degree, or a combination. Many come through the **Air Traffic Collegiate Training Initiative (AT-CTI)** college programs, which the FAA has expanded as "Enhanced AT-CTI." Prior military controllers have separate paths. New hires attend the **FAA Academy** at the Mike Monroney Aeronautical Center in Oklahoma City, then spend years in on-the-job training at their facility before reaching Certified Professional Controller (CPC) status. The age cap exists because controllers must retire at **56** (the Secretary can grant exemptions to 61 for exceptional skills). The FAA wants a full 20–25-year career before then, and controllers can retire at 50 with 20 years or at any age with 25.

**Washout rates.** The DOT Inspector General reported in February 2026 that the Academy failure rate "was over 30 percent for trainees" in FY2024, and opened an audit of instructor shortages, training capacity, an outdated curriculum and failure rates. The FAA said it agreed "the previous washout rate at the academy is unacceptable" and says success rates have since improved. Further attrition happens at facilities, especially busy ones like the New York TRACON, whose low training success rate was a stated reason for moving Newark airspace to Philadelphia.

**Pay.** BLS reports a May 2025 median of **$148,080** ($71.19/hour). The bottom 10% earned under $78,420 and the top 10% over $215,610. Federal-government controllers specifically had a median of $156,250. FAA pay depends on facility complexity and certification stage, not years served. Trainees at the Academy earn far less; one 2026 compilation puts Academy pay at $48,251. Base pay is capped by statute, and figures cited for the cap range from $228,000 to top-of-band numbers near $245,754 with locality pay, so treat specific ceilings as approximate. Overtime and night and Sunday differentials come on top of base pay.

**Stress and fatigue.** BLS notes the work "can be stressful because maximum concentration is required at all times." The FAA's own 2026–2028 workforce plan states plainly that "chronic use of overtime leads to fatigue, controller burnout and ultimately loss of retention." A National Academies of Sciences report, cited by Reuters, found the controller workforce logged 2.2 million hours of overtime in 2024 costing $200 million, with annual overtime per controller up 308% since 2013, to 167 hours on average. The labor-relations side is tense too. In August 2026 the FAA moved to fire the two LaGuardia controllers who left an hour early on the night of the collision. Reporting says the practice, known internally as an "early shove," is not uncommon because so many controllers work six-day weeks, though it is a form of timecard fraud. NATCA said it was in discussions with FAA leadership.

**The staffing shortage, by the numbers:**
- **Headcount:** GAO (GAO-26-107320) counted 13,164 controllers at the end of FY2025, down from 14,007 at the end of FY2015, while flights rose about 10% to 30.8 million.
- **Certified controllers:** Per the FAA, as of April 2026 approximately 11,000 CPCs were deployed across more than 300 facilities, with an additional 4,000 controllers in the training pipeline, including 1,000 who were previously fully certified.
- **The target moved.** The May 2026 workforce plan cut the full-staffing target to **12,563** CPCs, from the 14,633 the FAA forecast in 2024. The FAA says modern scheduling tools and a staffing model reviewed by the National Academies' Transportation Research Board justify the change. Critics call it moving the goalposts: on paper it closes more than 2,000 positions of the gap without a single hire.
- **Hiring:** GAO counts 2,026 trainees hired in FY2025 (the FAA cites 2,028), the most since 2008, and the FAA hired a record **2,235** in FY2026 against a 2,200 goal. Targets are 2,300 for FY2027 and 2,400 for FY2028. A March 2025 hiring window drew more than 10,000 applications and sent about 600 trainees into the Academy in a single month.
- **Shutdown shock:** During the 43-day fall 2025 government shutdown, about 13,000 controllers worked without pay and absences rose. The FAA ordered airlines to cut flights at 40 high-traffic airports, ramping from 4% with a planned 10%, and held at 6% as controllers returned. In one weekend, at least 39 facilities reported potential staffing limits.

**Aging equipment and modernization.** The DOT says "most of our radars date back to the 1980s." The Newark outages were traced to copper telecom lines carrying radar data from Long Island to Philadelphia. The FAA switched that link to a new fiber network in 2025, and the DOT Inspector General opened an audit of the relocation. The administration's **Brand New Air Traffic Control System (BNATCS)**, announced May 8, 2025, targets completion by the end of 2028. It is funded by a $12.5 billion down payment in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, and DOT is lobbying for about $20 billion more. Peraton was named prime integrator in December 2025. The scope covers more than 5,000 high-speed network connections, 27,625 radios, 462 digital voice switches, 612 new radars (initial contracts to RTX and Indra), surface surveillance upgrades, and the first new consolidated en route center since the 1960s. As of April 2026, the FAA reported new surface surveillance at 54 airports. These are goals, not delivered results. The Eno Center notes the funding plan is incomplete and that Peraton had no prior experience as an FAA prime integrator, so treat the 2028 date as ambitious.

**The two big accident reviews:**
- **DCA, January 29, 2025:** An American Eagle CRJ700 and an Army Black Hawk collided over the Potomac, killing all 67 aboard both aircraft. The NTSB's final report (board meeting January 27, 2026) found no single cause. It cited Helicopter Route 4's placement, FAA failure to act on prior warnings, overreliance on visual separation, the tower combining helicopter and local control during a busy period, and limits of both aircraft's collision-avoidance equipment. The NTSB approved 74 findings and 50 recommendations, 33 of them to the FAA and eight to the U.S. Army. A separate Army investigation has emphasized faulty altimeter readings on the helicopter.
- **LGA, March 22, 2026:** See the runway incursion case above. The NTSB investigation is ongoing, with only preliminary findings released, so causal conclusions are not final.

## Recommendations

- **If you want to understand the job viscerally:** Listen to archived ATC audio (for example, LiveATC recordings for a busy TRACON during an evening push) while watching a flight tracker. Then read FAA Order JO 7110.65 chapter 5 (radar separation). It reads like an RFC.
- **If you're considering the career:** Check your age eligibility first; the under-31 hiring cutoff is hard for civilians. Prepare for the AT-SA, and consider an Enhanced AT-CTI school. Expect 2–5 years from hire to full certification, a meaningful chance of washing out, rotating shifts for decades, and a facility assignment you may not choose. The pay and the 25-year pension window are the payoff.
- **If you're reading the news as a systems person:** The pattern in DCA, Newark and LGA is reduced redundancy under load: combined positions, a single copper link, a vehicle without a transponder. Judge modernization claims by whether they restore redundancy (fiber diversity, surface surveillance, staffed split positions), not just by new hardware counts.

## Caveats

- Several numbers here come from secondary reporting of FAA documents (for example, overtime hours and costs, and the four-to-five-hours time-on-position goal), not the primary plan text. Pay-cap figures vary by source.
- The FAA's staffing target reduction is contested. "Short by ~1,500" versus "short by ~3,500" depends on which target you use.
- BNATCS figures are plans and contract scopes, not completed deployments.
- The LaGuardia investigation is not final. Statements about controller actions there reflect NTSB preliminary information and press reports.
- Daily North Atlantic traffic figures vary by source and season; NATS's "two to three thousand" is the most authoritative range found.
